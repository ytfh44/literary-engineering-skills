# Dialogue Voice Integrity

## Use when

A character says a proposition they may plausibly understand, but in words they would not plausibly own.

## Do not use when

Do not equate youth, low education, dialect, or informal speech with weak reasoning. A child can infer sharply; a mechanic can think abstractly; a professor can swear. The issue is ownership of the sentence, not prestige.

## Core test

**Who would naturally own this sentence?**

If the best answer is “the author explaining the character,” move the proposition out of the character's mouth or rebuild it in the character's operational vocabulary.

## Workflow

1. Separate **proposition** from **register**. What does the character actually know or want?
2. Mark imported abstractions: therapy terms, management nouns, critic language, polished causal summaries.
3. Check age, occupation, local jargon, social relation, stress level, and what the character is trying to obtain **now**.
4. Re-express the proposition through nouns and verbs the character handles routinely.
5. Keep unusual sophistication when the story has earned it through habit, obsession, training, or mimicry.
6. Recheck nearby characters. If all of them use the same relation vocabulary, the narrator may be leaking through everyone.

## Minimal pair

> “我觉得我们需要重新审视一下彼此的边界。”

> “你别再拿我钥匙。”

For many characters, both can point to the same conflict. The second owns an object and an action. The first is right only for a speaker who actually talks in that conceptual register.

## Counterexamples

- A teenager who spends all day on therapy TikTok may naturally say “边界感”; age alone is not a veto.
- A frightened engineer may blurt a precise technical diagnosis because technical nouns are their shortest path to action.

## Return shape

Return **proposition -> current owner -> likely character owner -> register repair**. When revising dialogue, preserve the character's reasoning even when simplifying diction.
