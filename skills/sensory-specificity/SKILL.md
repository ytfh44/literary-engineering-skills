# Sensory Specificity

## Use when

A sentence gives the reader a conclusion where an object, action, measurement, or local analogy could carry more useful uncertainty.

## Do not use when

Do not enforce “show, don't tell.” A direct state label can be the cheapest truthful sentence. Do not replace one cliché with `皱眉`, `攥拳`, `呼吸一滞`, or `指尖发白` merely because these are visible.

## Core test

**Offer evidence with a bounded semantic domain: several nearby readings remain legal, irrelevant readings do not.**

Specificity is not detail count. One well-chosen event can constrain interpretation more than five facial features.

## Workflow

1. Find the state label or atmospheric conclusion.
2. Ask what observable difference that state makes **here**.
3. Choose one object, action, timing, threshold, or measure that carries the difference.
4. Check its domain: does it admit a small family of plausible readings rather than one sealed diagnosis or unlimited vagueness?
5. Delete generic supporting cues that repeat the same inference.
6. Keep the direct label when it is faster, belongs to the voice, or prevents a harmful ambiguity.

## Minimal pair

> 她显得很不耐烦。
>
> 她看了第三次表。

The second does not “show” impatience by rule. It supplies a timed action that supports impatience, anxiety, urgency, or calculation while excluding many unrelated states. Context chooses among the nearby readings.

## Counterexamples

- `他很累。` may be exact after a long action sequence; adding a catalogue of drooping eyelids can dilute it.
- `她攥紧拳头。` is not automatically superior to `她生气了`; clenched fists are generic and can mean pain, cold, or resolve.

## Return shape

Return **named state -> scene consequence -> inference range -> keep/replace reason**. Offer one or two evidence candidates, not an inventory of gestures.
