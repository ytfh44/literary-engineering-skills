# Sentence Pressure and Rhythm

## Use when

The sentence lengths may look varied while the information arrives at one dead speed, or a long sentence keeps adding clauses without one continuous path.

## Do not use when

Do not prescribe “alternate long and short sentences.” Length is an output. Pressure comes from when the reader receives, withholds, revises, or resolves information.

## Core test

**Rhythm is the timing of information, not the histogram of sentence lengths.**

## Workflow

1. Mark each sentence's new propositions.
2. Mark uncertainty introduced and uncertainty released.
3. Identify the sentence's load-bearing path: action, attention, argument, inventory, or interruption.
4. Split when one sentence carries unrelated paths.
5. Join when several short sentences fracture one continuous event.
6. Keep repetition only if each recurrence changes pressure, expectation, or scale.
7. Read punctuation as scheduling: where is the reader forced to wait?

## Pressure operations

- **accumulate** — each clause adds a constraint before release;
- **delay** — postpone the decisive noun/verb;
- **cut** — terminate a path early;
- **echo** — repeat with changed consequence;
- **release** — resolve the question a prior clause opened;
- **reset** — clear one scene unit before the next.

## Minimal pair

A: `他开门。走进去。看见桌上的杯子。停住了。`

B: `他开门走进去，直到看见桌上的杯子才停住。`

B is not better because it is longer. One continuous motion now carries a delayed stopping condition. If each step needs separate hesitation, A may be right.

## Counterexamples

- A sequence of short sentences can sustain pressure when each closes one escape route.
- A long inventory sentence can be right when simultaneity and accumulation are the point, even without a dramatic release.

Return **information path -> pressure failure -> boundary change -> predicted reading change**. Do not justify a revision with “more varied sentence lengths.”
