# Predicate Licensing and Personification

## Use when

A noun temporarily takes an event capacity its category usually lacks. The
sentence works — or fails — depending on how cheaply the reader recovers
the temporary role.

## Do not use when

Do not treat personification as a decoration to add for vividness, and do
not ban it as "unnatural." Neither reflex is engineering. If the scene needs
no boundary crossing, the literal predicate is not a missed opportunity.

## Core test

**What agent does this predicate normally require, and why can this noun
cross the boundary here?**

风追着他: 追 normally licenses a pursuer with intention. Wind has no
intention, yet the sentence survives — because the reader recovers "the
wind behaves as if pursuing," and the scene's geometry (gust, direction,
running figure) supports that reading without extra explanation.

## The two-part structure

Every working crossing is:

```text
nominal reclassification  — the noun is momentarily promoted to a class
                            that may carry the predicate (wind -> agent)
predicate licensing       — the predicate's requirements are temporarily
                            granted (intention, capacity, perception)
```

Neither part alone is the effect. The crossing fails when one part is
unsupported: the noun has no path to the new class, or the predicate's
requirements cannot be read as borrowed.

## Workflow

1. Write the literal event underneath: who actually does what?
2. Name the predicate's normal agent requirements (intention, control,
   perception, motion capacity, volition).
3. Identify the reclassification: what class is the noun temporarily
   promoted to?
4. Measure recovery cost: can the reader restore the literal event before
   noticing the unusual collocation? If explanation is needed after the
   sentence, the crossing failed its compression test.
5. Check what the crossing buys: new event structure, viewpoint, affect,
   or motif — not just strangeness.
6. Decide keep / literalize / recast by cost and gain.

## Minimal pair

> 风追着他跑过巷子。
>
> 风把他往巷子里推。

Both license the wind as a force. The first asks the reader to borrow
pursuit intention (costly, and the gain is mostly atmosphere). The second
borrows only force and direction — a smaller crossing with the same scene
geometry. The cheaper crossing is not "more literary"; it is the one whose
borrowed requirement the scene already supports.

> 城市醒了。
>
> 城市在清晨的公交声里醒了过来。

The second earns the same metaphor by giving the reclassification a
perceptible mechanism (sound as waking). The first may still work if the
surrounding prose supplies the mechanism; if it does not, the crossing is
unsupported.

## Counterexamples

- 门犹豫了一下才开: the hesitation is the scene's fact (someone hesitating
  behind the door); the crossing is cheap because the reader reassigns the
  hesitation to the person — the door is a relay, not an agent.
- 雪把声音接住了: the reclassification is acoustic (absorbing as catching);
  recovery is instant when the scene is about quiet. The crossing carries a
  real mechanism.
- A forced crossing that needs a gloss ("仿佛有什么力量在……") has failed:
  the explanation is the cost report.

## Rule strength

**Event decomposition.** No preference for or against personification, no
"animate the inanimate for vividness" rule, no ban on agentive coercion.
The reliable operations are: recover the literal event, enumerate the
predicate's agent requirements, price the reclassification, and test
recovery cost as a one-variable intervention. The verdict is cost- and
gain-based, per sentence.

## Return shape

Return **literal event -> predicate requirements -> reclassification ->
recovery cost -> gain -> keep/literalize/recast reason**. Do not praise a
crossing for being unusual; the price is the reader's recovery work.
